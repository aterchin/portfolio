## Context

A global pharma aesthetics brand runs social accounts in dozens of markets — US, Mexico, Germany, and more. Each region operates on its own, but the brand needed to see who's ahead and who's lagging.

The tool: pull metrics from Sprout Social, score each profile against its peers, show the results in something brand managers can actually use.

<Aside variant="info" title="Stack">
  Laravel Breeze + React via **Inertia.js**. Laravel handles routing; React renders on the client. ApexCharts for charts, Sprout Social API for data.
</Aside>

## How the scoring works

Eight metrics per profile for a date range:

- engagement rate, likes, comments, video views, and others

Each metric gets a 0–100 score using a **logarithmic, peer-relative** formula — you're compared to other profiles in the dataset, not some external benchmark.

The overall `rating` is a flat average of those eight scores. Profiles rank by rating within the dataset.

Detail views use donut and bar charts for per-metric breakdowns, color-coded by band:

- **0–40:** red
- **41–70:** yellow
- **71–100:** green

The main table shows the rating plus raw numbers — likes, comments, views. Per-metric scores drive the charts, not the table.

## What the scores actually mean

This is relative benchmarking, not a grade.

A score of 80 means you're beating most peers **in this dataset**. It does not mean you're objectively good at social media.

That matters:

- You can score 85 while doing badly — if everyone else is worse
- The same profile can jump from 45 to 65 with zero real change — a peer had a big month and shifted the distribution
- Log scaling keeps one outlier from dominating, but scores only mean something compared to each other

For a global brand, that's the point. It answers "which regions are lagging?" not "is Germany good at Instagram?" Different questions.

## Gaps

No guidance layer. Germany at 42 means Germany is behind peers — not "post more video" or "fix response time."

Per-metric scores exist but live in charts, not as action items. If video views are killing the average, that should be visible and labeled — not buried in a donut chart. The data's already there; it's a presentation problem, not a technical one.

We tried weighting metrics — engagement rate over raw likes, since it normalizes for audience size — but landed on a flat average. Simpler to sell internally.
